A review of Battle Angel Alita

DISCLAIMER: yet to be finished!

Given that my journey of discovery of anime and manga started out with a number of classic cyberpunk works, it was predictable that I would soon hear about Battle Angel Alita, although it was not until about a decade later that I could finally pick it up and read it until the end.

I find it interesting to first address why it took me so long. The simple reason is that Alita is not really cyberpunk; as a person coming from Ghost in the Shell and Serial Experiments Lain, this manga looked and felt nothing like my favorite stories. To best describe it to the uninitiated, Battle Angel Alita could be said to be much closer to Mad Max, Fallout, or to other post-apocalyptic works than any established cyberpunk universe; in the manga world, I would say its closest relative is Akira, given the abundance in both of body horror and the apocalyptic settings.

In fact, I do not even see Battle Angel Alita as cyberpunk at all, but rather as biopunk through and through.

Biopunk is often confused with cyberpunk because their backdrops tend to be fairly similar: near-future settings, dystopian systems, social decay, pervasive corporate control, free market forces running amok. The difference is in what constitutes the fundamental medium for control: information technology, or biotechnology. Furthering the confusion, many science fiction works combine the two; in Ghost in the Shell, information technology takes center stage, but body modification is pervasive and central to the world building.

As I see it, Battle Angel Alita is as close to biopunk as you can get. Information technology plays no meaningful part; computer exist, but simply as background props. We do see an AI at some point, but it feels more like a metaphysical entity than the product of computer technology. Body modification, however, is omnipresent and thematic; Alita herself is an entirely artificial creature, whose only remnant of her biological origins is a tiny portion of her brain. Invasive and crude body modification is an everyday sight in the Scrapyard; in Zalem, it is just as present, albeit seamless and transparent.

In fact, Battle Angel Alita takes a step further and depicts not just a biopunk world, but a post-apocalyptic one, not only in the material sense that the world and humanity as we know them have collapsed, but also in the abstract one that the pervasive, species-changing effects of technology are no longer a source for conflict, doubt, or speculation. Technology simply exists, it has modified human life in a way both deep and irreversible, and nobody feels the need to question any of that.

Biopunk, then, and not cyberpunk. Mad Max, Akira, and Fallout, not Ghost in the Shell. When I accepted to take Alita on its own terms, I discovered what is arguably one of the best biopunk titles in manga.

Battle Angel Alita spends precious little time detailing how the world has fallen. What could it have been? An ecological crisis, a global war, a runaway technological process? It is not very relevant, at least in the original run of the manga, which, unlike the successive entries in the franchise, consciously (and wisely) decided to keep the scope more focused, more personal, more centered on Alita's struggles to grow, understand herself and the people around her, find out that love has to coexist with hate, caring with indifference, and finally find a lingering hope for humanity.

Therefore, little to no background. The protagonist really is Alita, and not its dystopian world. Events begin with Alita's seemingly lifeless carcass finding herself on a scrap heap, made out of the refuse flooding down from the floating city of Zalem, and forming the core of the principal setting of the story for the first half of the manga—the Scrapyard.

Alita's introduction to humanity is, mercifully, a gentle one: she is picked up by the meek Ido, a doctor specializing in cyborgs who is also, as is later revealed, an exile from Zalem. It is no exaggeration to say that, in a barbarized world in which everyday life for surface-dwellers is characterized by cruelty, injustice, and suffering, Alita's own heart remains capable of love thanks to the tender care of Ido, a figure both fatherly and brotherly. Ido himself, it turns out, has not been left unscathed by the barbarity surrounding him: he moonlights as a freelance bounty hunter, disposing of the wreckage of humanity that a broken world like that of the Scrapyard's is bound to produce. His grim side hustle, together with his daily exposure to the warped combinations of steel and flesh of his cyborg patients, have not produced the effect of desensitizing him the world, but rather that of making him pine ever more strongly for a semblance of beauty in a life characterized by its opposite: Alita.

Alita, however, is hardly the newborn baby Ido would like to make of her. He wants to see in her an eternal emblem of innocence, destined to forever stay at his side as the only glimmer of real happiness in a world that is anything but. Alita, however, has a past; a deeply buried one, one she herself sees only faint flickers of. Her gentle and loving personality is accompanied by a strong sense of duty, of natural indignation to injustice, of wonderment at the world; she is resolute and strong-willed, determined to move up in the world, to understand it, and to find the last remaining embers of beauty in its creaks. She herself was the product of injustice, mercilessly thrown down to earth, her life hanging by a thread. The lingering silhouette of the floating city of Zalem, its utopia made possible only by a unscrupulous exploitation of what remains of life on the surface, serves as a constant reminder of how injustice is not just the texture, but the essence of the world.

Alita, it turns out, had been no ordinary girl before her fall to earth. She gradually discovers in her an innate ability to fight that she could not possibly have been simply born with: in spite of her small frame, she can take on opponents much bigger than her head-on. The secret to her unlikely success is a technique called Panzerkunst, a kind of martial arts hailing from Mars, a land known for the vicious, centuries-long conflict raging on its surface. With each suddenly remembered move, each resurfacing memory of a long forgotten past, Alita realizes she had originally been a kind of cyborg warrior, a war-scarred, disciplined soldier partaking in a brutal conflict between Earth and Mars. In a fateful last battle, she found herself hurled down to the surface of the Earth, with most of her body destroyed in the process.

Still, it was not the gradual return of Alita's past self alone that drove her to journey out in the world. Rather, it was a traumatic episode that taught her both a different kind of love than the one she experienced with Ido, and also how suddenly the indifference and cruelty of the world can crush it with hardly a second thought. Alita's small world changes forever when, one day, she meets a teenaged young boy called Hugo. For the standards of Alita's world, the setting is almost idyllic: the two meet in the outskirts of the city, in what seems to be an abandoned factory far away from the congested crucible of steel and flesh that forms the heart of the Scrapyard. Calm reigns, and it feels like the old Earth is back; a remnant of 20th century Earth, hardly its most beautiful one, but soothingly familiar compared with the alien hostility of the rest of the world.

Though Alita does not quite know, at first, how to describe this new feeling she had never felt before, it gradually becomes clear to her it is a form of attachment substantially different, though just as intense, as that she feels for Ido. Ido is, for her, a kind of loving father, a sort of protective elder brother, and her devotion to him is absolute, just as his is to her. Ido is the first face she got to know in her new life, a person feeling nothing but affection for her, and interested in nothing but her well-being; it would hardly be surprising if she had never met such a person in the gauntlet of her former life. Hugo, however, represents something different: someone youthful, innocent, yet-untouched by the corrupting influence of a terminally ill Earth. The moments she shares with him are nothing but joyous, entirely lost in the present, unencumbered by troubling thoughts about life at large.

Yet not all is as it seems. It is not realistically possible to escape the gravitational pull of a world like Alita's. Hugo really is naive, playful, at times even innocent, but that is simply a fortunate byproduct of his youth; however, the rot of his surroundings has already started taking hold in him. Hugo has a simple dream: he sees the decay of everything around him, he remains scarred by the way his family and, by extension, his carefree years ended, and then he sees Zalem: floating high above in the sky, seemingly untouched by whatever is plaguing the surface of the planet. A utopia. And Hugo wants there, too.

You need money to get to Zalem, however, or so he has been told. A lot of money. He finds a clever way to raise it, and fairly quickly, too: with the help of two other teenagers, he pretends to offer lubrication work to cyborgs, and, once the victim has been snared, proceeds to paralyze it and steal their spinal columns. He does not have too many moral qualms about it; he is not killing his victims, after all, as a cyborg's spinal column can be replaced (if you can make it to a clinic, that is).
