A review of Ergo Proxy

What an unexpected surprise this show turned out to be. You never know when something might finally do it for you; in Ergo Proxy's case, that took multiple failed watching attempts until, this time, I was firmly gripped by the very first episode.

What is Ergo Proxy, anyway? I realize today, after having reached the end of the series, that comparisons with it tend to be quite misleading. You often hear it referred to as a cyberpunk anime. Well, is it? Is it something akin to Ghost in the Shell? It sure feels like that, to an extent, in the very first episodes. Well, you will not be thinking that anymore halfway through it. Is it something closer to Serial Experiments Lain? Quite possibly, yes, since it shares some of the non-linear, fragmented narrative Lain is so famous for. Ultimately, though, it is entirely its own creature.

Without further ado, let us get a couple of common criticisms out of the way. I heard, over time, opinions claiming that Ergo Proxy is too confusing, too indulgently philosophical, too heavily reliant on ambiguity and an unsteady narrative to get its point across.

First, while there is no question the show is inspired by certain real-life philosophical and religious movements, not to mention certain thinkers, you hardly need to know any of those to catch on to the show's plot.

Furthermore, the show's unorthodox approach to storytelling, its many surreal or dreamy sequences, are by design. Learn to embrace them early on, and get carried away by the story. Ghost in the Shell, or any other techno-thriller, works by being very direct and open about its plot; the confusion comes from the sheer complexity of its world and the intricate politics taking place in it (just like our world!). Ergo Proxy does not work the same way; it takes place in a world where the human element is no longer dominant, and, just as human society has faded away, so has the clarity and intelligibility that comes with it. Sense and meaning are inherent human traits, and they are to be found only where our consciousness prevails; if you take the latter out of the picture, the surrounding world reverts to its original, inscrutable self.

The show opens in Romdo, a domed city that looks like your typical cyberpunk-esque utopia, though it will not take you long to see that it really is a dystopia in disguise. A perfectly clean, sterile, and orderly haven from an outside world that has become unfit for human existence. Within Romdo, new human life is produced within artificial wombs, assigned a role at birth, and encouraged—better yet, required—to think of nothing but fulfilling one's lot in life and consuming; incredibly advanced androids called AutoReivs assist human beings in their day-to-day tasks. Overseeing it all is a totalitarian, technocratic system headed by a comatose elderly Regent, whose remnants of consciousness are now channeled through four specialized AutoReiv statues (incidentally based on four real-world sculptures by Michelangelo), and devoted to nothing but the upkeep of this beautiful glass house.

The social contract of this artificial paradise in which freedom and unpredictability are expunged for the sake of stability and survival is simple: give up control of your life to the State, and the latter will ensure you lead a comfortable existence, untroubled by the reality of the outside world.

Yet, no sooner are we introduced to this gilded cage than we begin to see the cracks running through it. Apparently, swaths of immigrants have been streaming into Romdo from other domes that have fallen into ruin. Is the worldwide dome network failing? Furthermore, increasing numbers of Romdo's AutoReivs, the underpinning of much of the city's efficiency, are being infected by the mysterious Cogito virus, which has the effect of making them stray from their programming and turn rogue. In fact, the infection has been spreading so fast that the city's authorities are scrambling to determine its causes, and many of the newly arrived immigrants are enlisted to directly hunt down and kill the infected androids. Finally, dark experiments have apparently been taking place deep in the bowels of Romdo; some kind of monstrous creature, referred to as a Proxy, has broken free from its shackles and is now running amok throughout the city.

It is precisely at the intersection of these mushrooming crises that we meet the protagonists of the show. Re-l Mayer, the beautiful and rebellious granddaughter of the Regent, Donov Mayer, and agent of the Intelligence Bureau, tasked with investigating the Cogito virus outbreak; Vincent Law, the meek and unassuming immigrant from Mosk employed by the AutoReiv Disposal Unit to hunt down and kill rogue androids; Raul Creed, the ambitious and charismatic chief of the Security Bureau, who, soon after having been appointed, is forced to face the twofold crisis of the escaped Proxy and the Cogito virus; Daedalus Yumeno, the brilliant and sharp chief of the Health and Welfare Bureau, officially charged with the maintenance of the artificial womb system, but who clearly has a very significant role in the experiments conducted on the Proxy that later broke free.

Re-l Mayer stands out immediately as a natural protagonist for the show. Her very attire, hairstyle and makeup immediately set her apart from the rest of the dome's population, and accentuate her rebellious, unconventional personality. You will find nobody else in the show wearing the same dark gothic clothing, the striking blue eyeshadow, and subtly unconventional hairstyle; not even the elegant Raul can compare. Re-l makes no attempt to hide her disdain for the dull, uneventful life in the dome; it is therefore hardly surprising that, once the events that are destined to unravel the dome's peaceful existence start to unfold, she makes it her priority to pursue the truth about the world.

As for Vincent Law, you would be forgiven for not thinking much of him the way he is presented to us at the beginning of the show. It is clear he is to play some important role later on; after all, the opening animation suggests as much. His baggy red clothes, which he shares with all other immigrants, his timid and submissive demeanor, his closed eyes, his slicked back hair, all seem to indicate an unremarkable if kind personality that will hardly play a determining role in the story. Yet, suffice it to say that there is a very good reason behind this first impression. Keep an eye out, pun intended, on those eyes of his.

Oh, and Pino. How could I forget about Pino? While we are not introduced to her right at the outset, we soon will be; at first, she looks like a very cute robotic little girl that was assigned to Raul’s family as a surrogate child. As an AutoReiv, despite her faithful human-like appearance, she is devoid of a will of her own. Furthermore, she is really good at playing the piano and drawing; she is supposed to be a good daughter, is she not? How ironic, then, that this android, whom Raul’s wife wants to dispose of now that her family has been assigned a natural, albeit artificially-born child, will soon enough become the emotional anchor of the series after accidentally getting infected with the Cogito virus and developing a soul easily surpassing that of many human characters in the story.

In spite of Romdo’s best attempts to keep the illusion of its sheltered existence intact, the stark reality of the world will soon prove too strong to contain. The world as a whole has entered into an irreversible crisis that goes far beyond the outbreak of the Cogito virus, which is a mere symptom of it. Could those two mysterious monsters, referred to as Proxies, that have been surreptitiously wandering about Romdo and that the domed city’s authorities have taken such a keen interest in, have anything to do with it?

While an outright breakdown of Romdo’s fragile order has yet to arrive, the cracks in its foundation have become too hard to ignore. After a particularly fateful encounter, Re-l is no longer able to close her eyes to it—not that she ever wanted to, given how insufferable she found Romdo’s previous condition to be. Vincent, on the contrary, would by far prefer the previous uneventfulness of his life to continue, desiring nothing more than to keep fulfilling his allotted tasks with the goal of becoming a model citizen—but reality does not intend to abide by his torpor. Our tender little Pino, on the other hand, nonchalantly stumbles upon Vincent as the latter is desperately trying to escape from the thread that fate is weaving for him. Thus, the seeds have been sown for an unlikely trio to be born. No one wanted it—well, with the possible exception of Re-l—but it is those three that will have to face, firsthand, the dark truth behind their reality, and the ominous future that will soon come to pass.

It is at this point that the show reveals its true intentions. The focus quickly shifts from the city of Romdo to the desolate wasteland surrounding it. Make no mistake, Romdo will remain a central location throughout the rest of the story. Nevertheless, before uncovering its deepest secrets, our protagonists must first understand themselves. It turns out there is more to the gentle Vincent than meets the eye. What has he been running away from all this time? Might it be his real self? Might his immigrant persona be but a ruse? As for Re-l, why is it that she feels so irresistibly drawn to the mystery behind Vincent, if not Vincent himself? Is it just her curiosity to seek out the truth, or is there more to it?

Gosh, how overbearing it can all feel sometimes. It is a good thing we have Pino keeping us grounded; you would not have thought the AutoReiv would have been the sweetest character of all, would you?

Ultimately, Ergo Proxy is doubtless a rare breed of anime. It has a haunting, melancholy presence that will refuse to leave you even once the final episode has come to a close. If you are the right kind of person, make sure not to miss this experience.
