Evolving Dystopia: Cyberpunk and Biopunk

Neither cyberpunk nor biopunk is merely an aesthetic. The two genres share the fundamental idea of technological evolution outpacing human meaning, a force leading not to mankind's betterment, but to its decay. They belong to the wider family of dystopian science fiction that has become dominant starting in the 20th century as a response to a historically unprecedented acceleration in technological progress, the radical social transformations that ensued, and dramatic historical events. One need only look at classic titles such as Brave New World or 1984, in which technology, industrialized society, and ideology combine to create a dystopian society that feels like a disturbingly plausible culmination of our own historical trajectory.

Cyberpunk and biopunk take a step further compared to traditional dystopian science fiction; whereas the latter depicts technology as a mere tool that, when wielded by corrupt power, can be misused to produce dystopia, the former show that technology can develop into forms that are structurally alienating and inhuman, independently of the power structures that enable it.

Two examples may help elucidate the point.

In Fahrenheit 451, society is engineered around conformity and the suppression of thought; people are kept distracted through wall-sized TV screens and seashell radios whispering constant noise in their ears. With all buildings now fire-proof, firemen ignite fires rather than extinguish them, for the purpose of destroying all books carrying any memory of the past. Though the world depicted is technologically sophisticated, the true source of dystopia lies not in the technology itself but in the ideology that wields it: one of censorship, thought control, and anti-intellectualism. Technology remains instrumental to the political power that wields it.

In Gattaca, eugenics is widespread, and people are, at birth, grouped between those who are less and those who are more susceptible to genetic disorders. Though genetic discrimination is illegal, one's genotype fundamentally alters the trajectory of their life, as those who are "valid" are allowed to enter the higher rungs of society, while those who are "less valid" are systematically held back. Eugenics, it turns out, is inherently alienating, no matter the intentions of the political system; it inevitably creates a world where human worth is biologically predetermined. Certain technological trajectories, therefore, produce dystopia by their very nature, not through misuse.

Certain technological trajectories are, therefore, inherently dystopian, while others are not. Communication technology in Fahrenheit 451 becomes dystopian only because the flow of information is weaponized for thought control; if left unconstrained, it can just as easily become a tool of empowerment. Eugenics in Gattaca, by contrast, inevitably leads to dystopia because it necessarily creates a biological hierarchy, rendering society fundamentally unequal.

Cyberpunk and biopunk are therefore alike in how technology is not an instrument, but the very substrate of dystopia. Where they differ is in what the technology is.

Cyberpunk's medium of alienation is information technology. Through it, identity is mediated through networks, and the self, unmoored from its increasingly irrelevant body, ends up dissolving in it.

Biopunk's medium of alienation is biological technology. Through it, the boundaries of the body are violated, and the instability of the body leads to the instability of identity, the self finding itself invaded, rewritten, commodified.

Cyberpunk dissolves the self outward; biopunk violates the self inward.

Pure cyberpunk is exceedingly rare, as it is often combined with elements of biopunk. Of the few examples one can make, Snow Crash is a prominent one: in its world, no forms of body modification take place, and information technology takes center stage. In Snow Crash, computers have become advanced enough to create a network similar to the Internet as we know it, but based on virtual reality, where people can lead a genuine parallel life in a world that feels physical, externalizing their self in a digital avatar. For many, life in the Metaverse is the real life, the place where most of their personal, cultural, and professional lives occur. Reality becomes an afterthought.

Pure biopunk is surprisingly common. Even the foundational "cyberpunk" work, Blade Runner, which defined the genre's tropes and aesthetic, can be construed as such: in its universe, computers are mere props; digital networks, if any are present, do not structure the world. The fulcrum of Blade Runner's dystopia lies in the human body, specifically in humanity's ability to create artificial humans known as Replicants. What makes "natural" human existence more real when human life can be artificially engineered, implanted with artificial memories, and endowed with a soul? Human existence is entirely relativized by the existence of artificial human life that is not only biologically equivalent, but that also develops a soul in spite of not having been carried in a womb and having been implanted with artificial memories. Though the Replicants are practically entirely human, they are considered nothing but objects and endowed with extremely limited lifespans: this leads to their desperate attempt at clinging to life and finding a way to prolong it. However, the real philosophical core of the film is not in the Replicants themselves, but how their mere existence destabilizes the definition of what it means to be human: "natural life" becomes only one form of consciousness among many, and the self is forced to confront the terrifying possibility that its uniqueness was an illusion.

It is most common to see the two genres appearing at once: Ghost in the Shell can be considered the archetypical example.
